Automating the maintenance of the machines that build the world.

Heavy industry is the foundation of the modern economy. It mines the copper our data centers are built around, moves the freight behind same-day delivery, and harvests the food on our tables. All of it runs on machines that can't afford to go down. Right now, a haul truck is parked on a ramp in a mine waiting for a technician, and everything it was meant to move is stuck with it. That wait is getting longer, because there are fewer technicians every year. We are using physical AI and robotics to shorten that wait.

Manifesto

Robots are for doing work

Robotics is in its demo era. Humanoids folding laundry make good videos, but very few robots are doing real work at scale. We believe a robot should be judged on how much work it gets done without a person there, and the place to start is where the work matters most and there aren't enough people to do it.

The machines behind everything

Every tonne of copper, kilometre of highway, load of freight, and harvest moves through a haul truck, an excavator, a Class 8 tractor, or a combine. When one goes down, the work stops with it. Keeping those machines up is the job of a heavy equipment technician, and there are not enough of them.

The machines got harder to fix

Heavy equipment is now a network of computers. A modern truck or loader has many control units on a databus, emissions systems with their own sensors and software, and telematics streaming fault codes all day, read through a different proprietary tool for every brand. Diagnosis used to mean listening to an engine. Now it means reading data from a dozen systems, and the technicians best at it are retiring.

Where we start

A large share of a technician's week goes to predictable work like greasing, fluids, filters, and inspections. Robots should take that first, then AI should take on diagnosis, so the skilled people we have can focus on the hard problems.

The problem

More machines to keep running, fewer people to do it

Not enough technicians are coming in

Schools aren't producing enough technicians to replace the ones leaving. For diesel, the trade closest to heavy equipment, they graduate roughly one new technician for every two the industry needs. The BLS expects about 20,300 openings a year for US heavy vehicle and mobile equipment technicians, and most of those aren't new jobs. They are seats left empty by people retiring or walking away.

Every industry is short at once

No industry can fix this by hiring from another, because they are all short at once. Construction and farm equipment dealers are missing about 10,000 technicians a year in the US and 1,200 in Canada, and 80 percent say they can't meet customer demand. In trucking, 65.5 percent of diesel shops were understaffed in 2025. Mining needs about 191,000 hires in Canada alone over the next decade. Four industries are fishing in the same small pool, and every technician one of them hires is one another loses.

The experience is leaving

The people who know these machines best are the hardest to replace. The median heavy-duty technician has 17 years of experience, and only 17 percent of shop respondents are under 30. Even the ones who stay are looking around: 79 percent of equipment technicians would leave for another industry, and turnover peaks two to five years in, right when a technician is fully trained. So when a senior tech retires, a shop needs two new hires to replace them, and most can't even find one.

Why not hire more people?

Everyone is already trying. Wages rose more than 14 percent last year, dealers are recruiting overseas, and OEMs are funding schools, and the shortage is still growing. Hiring can't win this race. It takes years to train a technician who can diagnose a machine alone, and the machines are getting more complex faster than anyone can be trained on them. Training can't speed up and the machines won't get simpler, so the only thing that scales is automation.

Downtime is the real cost

Maintenance is 20 to 40 percent of operating cost in heavy industry, and it drives 30 to 50 percent of lost equipment effectiveness. When a haul truck or a tractor sits waiting for a technician, the cost isn't the repair. It is everything that machine was supposed to be doing. And when a shop is short, preventive maintenance is the first thing skipped, which causes the next breakdown.

Why we are building here

This is one of the few places in the economy where the demand for physical work is growing, the people to do it are disappearing, and nobody disputes it. Machines now produce enough data for a model to see what a technician sees, and robots can finally handle repetitive physical work in messy, real environments. The OEMs each cover only their own brand, and every fleet we have talked to runs a mix. Because we don't sell equipment, we can work on every machine on the site.
